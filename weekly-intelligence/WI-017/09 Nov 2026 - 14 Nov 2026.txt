*Chapter 01 – Distinguishing Task Completion from Successful Outcomes*

*Understanding*

When we complete a task, we often look for something visible that tells us the work is finished. A report has been written, a calculation has been performed, or a recommendation has been prepared. These results can show that an activity has taken place, but they do not always tell us whether the original purpose has been achieved.

Consider asking AI to recommend an educational institution. It may produce a detailed list with explanations and comparisons. The requested activity appears complete. But what if the institutions do not meet the learner's actual requirements, or important information has not been verified? The output exists, yet the intended outcome may remain unmet.

This distinction becomes increasingly important as AI systems begin carrying out several steps of work independently. We need to recognise that completing the requested actions and achieving the intended result are two different things.

*Practice*

Think of one task you recently completed yourself or with AI assistance. Identify what was produced at the end: a document, a decision, a calculation, a plan, or another visible result.

Now ask a different question: What was this task actually supposed to achieve?

Compare the finished work with that original purpose. Did the result genuinely satisfy the need, or did it simply demonstrate that the requested steps were performed? If something important remains unresolved, identify what is missing before considering the work complete.

*Looking Ahead*

Recognising the difference between activity and outcome changes how we approach responsibility. We begin looking beyond whether something was done and asking whether it achieved what mattered.

Tomorrow, we will explore how to define success before beginning or delegating a task, so that completion can be judged against a meaningful purpose rather than appearances alone.

*Chapter 02 – Defining What Success Should Look Like*

*Understanding*

Yesterday, we explored why completing a task is not always the same as achieving its intended outcome. Today, we take the next step: understanding what success should look like before the work begins.

When we ask someone or an AI system to complete a task, we often describe the activity we want performed. We may ask for a report, a comparison, a plan, or a solution. But the instruction alone may not explain what would make the result genuinely useful.

Consider asking AI to prepare a study plan. A timetable containing subjects and study hours may appear complete. Yet if the learner has limited time, struggles with particular concepts, or needs to prepare for a specific examination, a generic timetable may not achieve the intended purpose. Defining success means understanding those needs before deciding what a good result should contain.

*Practice*

Choose one task you intend to complete today, either independently or with AI assistance.

Before beginning, write one sentence explaining what you actually want to achieve. Then ask yourself: Who is this result for? What need should it satisfy? What would be different if the task were genuinely successful?

Try to describe success without simply naming the output. Instead of saying, “I need a completed study plan,” you might say, “I need a realistic plan that helps me prepare for my examination within the time available.”

Notice how the second description gives you a clearer purpose against which the completed work can later be judged.

*Looking Ahead*

When we define success before starting, we give ourselves a better way to recognise whether the work has achieved its purpose.

But understanding the goal is only the beginning. Tomorrow, we will examine how to turn that goal into clear requirements and acceptance criteria, so that important expectations are not overlooked when the work is carried out.

*Chapter 03 – Establishing Requirements and Acceptance Criteria*

*Understanding*

Yesterday, we learned to define what success should look like before beginning a task. But even when the goal is clear, we may still disagree about whether the finished work is acceptable. To make that judgment more reliable, we need to identify the requirements that must be satisfied.

A requirement describes something the work needs to include, respect, or achieve. An acceptance criterion gives us a way to determine whether that requirement has been met. Together, they help us move from a general expectation to something we can actually examine.

Consider asking AI to prepare a study plan for an examination. We may define success as helping a learner prepare effectively within the time available. But the plan also needs to respect the examination date, available study hours, important subjects, and areas where the learner needs additional practice. Without these requirements, even a well-presented timetable may fail to serve its purpose.

*Practice*

Choose a task you want to complete or delegate today. Begin by stating its intended outcome in one sentence.

Next, identify three requirements that the result must satisfy. For each requirement, write a simple question that would help you check whether it has been met.

For example, if the task is to prepare a study plan, one requirement might be that the schedule fits the learner's available time. The corresponding acceptance question would be: Can the learner realistically complete the planned activities within those hours?

Try to distinguish essential requirements from preferences. A result may look different from what we imagined while still being acceptable, but it should not overlook something necessary for achieving its purpose.

*Looking Ahead*

Clear requirements and acceptance criteria help us communicate expectations before work begins and evaluate results more consistently afterwards.

Tomorrow, we will explore the next challenge: examining the actual evidence of completed work. A requirement may be clearly stated, but we still need a reliable way to determine whether it has truly been satisfied.

*Chapter 04 – Examining Evidence of Completed Work*

*Understanding*

Yesterday, we learned how requirements and acceptance criteria help us describe what a successful result must satisfy. Today, we explore how to determine whether those conditions have actually been met.

When someone tells us that a task is complete, we may naturally trust the statement, especially when the finished work looks convincing. But a statement of completion is not the same as evidence that the intended outcome has been achieved.

Consider asking AI to compare educational institutions. It may return a detailed comparison containing fees, courses, facilities, and recommendations. The result may appear thorough, but how do we know that the information is accurate, current, and relevant to the learner's needs?

We can examine the sources, check important facts, compare the recommendations against the original requirements, and identify any details that remain unconfirmed. Evidence helps us distinguish between a result that appears satisfactory and one we have good reasons to accept.

*Practice*

Choose one completed task, whether produced by yourself, another person, or an AI system.

Identify one important claim about its success. For example, the claim might be that a calculation is correct, a report contains accurate information, or a proposed solution meets the stated requirements.

Now ask: What evidence supports this claim?

Look for something you can examine directly, such as a calculation you can reproduce, a reliable source you can check, a test result, or a comparison against an agreed requirement.

If the evidence is insufficient, do not immediately assume the work is wrong. Instead, identify what additional verification would help you make a better judgment.

*Looking Ahead*

Examining evidence allows us to move beyond simply accepting that work has been completed. It helps us understand why a result should or should not be trusted.

Tomorrow, we will explore what happens when the evidence is incomplete, when important uncertainties remain, or when the work introduces risks that cannot be ignored.

*Chapter 05 – Recognising Gaps, Uncertainty, and Unresolved Risks*

*Understanding*

Throughout this week, we have learned to distinguish task completion from successful outcomes, define what success means, establish acceptance criteria, and examine evidence. Today, we consider what happens when the evidence does not fully support a conclusion.

A task may appear nearly complete while still containing missing information, uncertain assumptions, or unresolved risks. These are not always obvious mistakes. Sometimes the available evidence is simply insufficient to determine whether the intended outcome has been achieved.

Consider asking AI to recommend a course of study. It may identify suitable programmes and explain their benefits. But what if current admission requirements have not been confirmed, the learner's financial constraints are unclear, or an important eligibility condition remains unknown? The recommendations may still be useful, but treating them as final could lead to a poor decision.

Recognising uncertainty does not mean rejecting everything that is incomplete. It means understanding what remains unknown, why it matters, and whether further action is necessary before accepting the result.

*Practice*

Choose a completed task or recommendation that you have recently reviewed.

Identify one piece of information that may be missing, one assumption that has not been confirmed, and one possible consequence if the result turns out to be incorrect.

Then consider which of these concerns genuinely matters to the intended outcome. Some gaps may be minor, while others could prevent the work from being accepted responsibly.

Decide what you would do next. Would you verify a fact, ask a clarifying question, revise part of the work, or delay the final decision until essential evidence becomes available?

The goal is to practise recognising uncertainty without confusing it with failure.

*Looking Ahead*

A responsible decision does not always mean declaring that the work is finished. Sometimes the right outcome is to identify what remains unresolved and explain what needs to happen next.

Tomorrow, we will bring the week's learning together by practising how to make and justify a final completion decision, including when to accept work, request corrections, or keep a task open.

*Looking Back*

This morning, we examined why recognising missing information, uncertain assumptions, and unresolved risks is essential when judging completed work.

A result can be valuable without being ready for final acceptance. Understanding this distinction helps us avoid two extremes: trusting every confident conclusion or rejecting useful work simply because some uncertainty remains.

The important question is whether the remaining uncertainty affects the purpose, reliability, or consequences of the result.

*Thinking Together*

Imagine an AI assistant has prepared a financial comparison for an important purchase. Most calculations are correct, the options are clearly presented, and the recommendation appears reasonable.

However, one important cost has not been confirmed.

Should the entire task be considered unsuccessful? Should the recommendation be accepted because most of the work is correct? Or should the result be treated as useful but incomplete until that cost is verified?

The answer depends on how important the missing information is to the decision. If it could substantially change the recommendation, accepting the result without further checking may be irresponsible.

This illustrates an important part of human judgment: we must understand not only what is known, but also what remains uncertain and how that uncertainty affects the next decision.

*Moving Forward*

As AI systems become more capable of performing complex work, we will increasingly need to distinguish between progress, provisional results, and genuinely completed outcomes.

Tomorrow, we will bring these ideas together by making a responsible completion decision based on purpose, requirements, evidence, and unresolved risks.
