*Module 02 – Judging Whether Work Has Truly Been Completed*

*Chapter 01 – Distinguishing Task Completion from Successful Outcomes*

*Understanding*

When we complete a task, we often look for something visible that tells us the work is finished. A report has been written, a calculation has been performed, or a recommendation has been prepared. These results can show that an activity has taken place, but they do not always tell us whether the original purpose has been achieved.

Consider asking AI to recommend an educational institution. It may produce a detailed list with explanations and comparisons. The requested activity appears complete. But what if the institutions do not meet the learner's actual requirements, or important information has not been verified? The output exists, yet the intended outcome may remain unmet.

This distinction becomes increasingly important as AI systems begin carrying out several steps of work independently. We need to recognise that completing the requested actions and achieving the intended result are two different things.

*Practice*

Think of one task you recently completed yourself or with AI assistance. Identify what was produced at the end: a document, a decision, a calculation, a plan, or another visible result.

Now ask a different question: What was this task actually supposed to achieve?

Compare the finished work with that original purpose. Did the result genuinely satisfy the need, or did it simply demonstrate that the requested steps were performed? If something important remains unresolved, identify what is missing before considering the work complete.

*Looking Ahead*

Recognising the difference between activity and outcome changes how we approach responsibility. We begin looking beyond whether something was done and asking whether it achieved what mattered.

Tomorrow, we will explore how to define success before beginning or delegating a task, so that completion can be judged against a meaningful purpose rather than appearances alone.

*Chapter 02 – Defining What Success Should Look Like*

*Understanding*

Yesterday, we explored why completing a task is not always the same as achieving its intended outcome. Today, we take the next step: understanding what success should look like before the work begins.

When we ask someone or an AI system to complete a task, we often describe the activity we want performed. We may ask for a report, a comparison, a plan, or a solution. But the instruction alone may not explain what would make the result genuinely useful.

Consider asking AI to prepare a study plan. A timetable containing subjects and study hours may appear complete. Yet if the learner has limited time, struggles with particular concepts, or needs to prepare for a specific examination, a generic timetable may not achieve the intended purpose. Defining success means understanding those needs before deciding what a good result should contain.

*Practice*

Choose one task you intend to complete today, either independently or with AI assistance.

Before beginning, write one sentence explaining what you actually want to achieve. Then ask yourself: Who is this result for? What need should it satisfy? What would be different if the task were genuinely successful?

Try to describe success without simply naming the output. Instead of saying, “I need a completed study plan,” you might say, “I need a realistic plan that helps me prepare for my examination within the time available.”

Notice how the second description gives you a clearer purpose against which the completed work can later be judged.

*Looking Ahead*

When we define success before starting, we give ourselves a better way to recognise whether the work has achieved its purpose.

But understanding the goal is only the beginning. Tomorrow, we will examine how to turn that goal into clear requirements and acceptance criteria, so that important expectations are not overlooked when the work is carried out.

*Chapter 03 – Establishing Requirements and Acceptance Criteria*

*Understanding*

Yesterday, we learned to define what success should look like before beginning a task. But even when the goal is clear, we may still disagree about whether the finished work is acceptable. To make that judgment more reliable, we need to identify the requirements that must be satisfied.

A requirement describes something the work needs to include, respect, or achieve. An acceptance criterion gives us a way to determine whether that requirement has been met. Together, they help us move from a general expectation to something we can actually examine.

Consider asking AI to prepare a study plan for an examination. We may define success as helping a learner prepare effectively within the time available. But the plan also needs to respect the examination date, available study hours, important subjects, and areas where the learner needs additional practice. Without these requirements, even a well-presented timetable may fail to serve its purpose.

*Practice*

Choose a task you want to complete or delegate today. Begin by stating its intended outcome in one sentence.

Next, identify three requirements that the result must satisfy. For each requirement, write a simple question that would help you check whether it has been met.

For example, if the task is to prepare a study plan, one requirement might be that the schedule fits the learner's available time. The corresponding acceptance question would be: Can the learner realistically complete the planned activities within those hours?

Try to distinguish essential requirements from preferences. A result may look different from what we imagined while still being acceptable, but it should not overlook something necessary for achieving its purpose.

*Looking Ahead*

Clear requirements and acceptance criteria help us communicate expectations before work begins and evaluate results more consistently afterwards.

Tomorrow, we will explore the next challenge: examining the actual evidence of completed work. A requirement may be clearly stated, but we still need a reliable way to determine whether it has truly been satisfied.

*Chapter 03 – Establishing Requirements and Acceptance Criteria*

*Looking Back*

This morning, we explored how to turn a desired outcome into requirements that can guide our work and acceptance criteria that help us judge the result.

Defining success tells us what we are trying to achieve. Requirements make that expectation more specific. Acceptance criteria help us determine whether the important conditions have actually been met.

These distinctions matter because a finished result may satisfy some expectations while overlooking others that are essential.

*Thinking Together*

Imagine two people reviewing the same completed project. One says the work looks good and should be accepted. The other notices that an important requirement has not been fulfilled.

Without clear acceptance criteria, their disagreement may depend largely on personal impressions. But when expectations were established beforehand, both can examine the result against the same standard.

This does not mean every decision can be reduced to a checklist. Some outcomes require interpretation, context, and human judgment. Clear criteria do not replace that judgment; they give it a more reliable foundation.

*Moving Forward*

As AI performs more complex tasks, we can improve the way we delegate work by communicating not only what should be produced, but also what conditions the result must satisfy.

Tomorrow, we will move from defining those conditions to examining the evidence. We will ask how we can know whether a requirement has actually been met rather than accepting a statement of completion at face value.
