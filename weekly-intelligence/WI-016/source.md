*Theme*

*AI Can Improve Our Performance Without Improving Our Skills*

*Why This Week?*

One of the biggest promises of AI is that it allows people to perform tasks they could not previously complete independently. Someone without programming experience can analyse data, a researcher can investigate unfamiliar scientific problems, and a professional can work across disciplines beyond their original expertise.

But an important question remains: when AI helps us perform something successfully, does it mean we have actually learned how to do it?

On October 26, research published in *Nature Human Behaviour* examined this question through a randomised controlled trial involving 487 management consultants. The findings revealed an important distinction: generative AI improved workers' ability to perform unfamiliar technical tasks, but researchers found no evidence that these improvements translated into independent technical knowledge after the experiment. [Wiles et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02566-6)

This deserves attention because AI can make us more capable in the moment without necessarily making us more capable in the long term. As AI becomes part of everyday learning and professional work, we must understand the difference between receiving assistance and developing lasting competence.

*1. Weekly Insight*

*The Difference Between Performing and Learning*

On October 27, Anthropic published early results from six partnerships involving AI-assisted drug development. These projects demonstrated how Claude could support different stages of scientific and operational work, from generating antibody designs to analysing laboratory data and preparing regulatory documentation. In one project, AI generated approximately 35,000 antibody designs in about a month for further scientific evaluation. In another, it helped reduce the review time for manufacturing documentation by 89%. [Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/early-progress-drug-development)

These examples demonstrate how AI can extend the productive capabilities of people working in highly specialised fields. However, the results also depended on scientific teams, laboratory testing, expert evaluation, and human decisions. Anthropic explicitly describes much of this work as early-stage and preclinical, not evidence of approved new treatments.

The broader development is that AI is increasingly allowing people to work beyond their existing capabilities. But there is an important distinction between completing a task with assistance and acquiring the knowledge needed to complete it independently. This distinction becomes central when we think about the future of education, professional development, and lifelong learning.

*2. Research Spotlight*

*Does AI Assistance Create Lasting Skills?*

The October 26 study by Emma Wiles and colleagues, published in *Nature Human Behaviour*, examined whether generative AI helps workers develop new technical skills.

Researchers conducted a randomised controlled trial involving 487 management consultants. Participants received access to ChatGPT and training to solve data science problems outside their existing areas of expertise. The researchers found that participants using AI performed better than the control group on three tasks. However, they could reject the hypothesis that AI-assisted participants performed as well as expert data scientists.

More importantly, after the experiment, researchers found no evidence that participants had developed stronger technical knowledge when answering questions without AI assistance. [Wiles et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02566-6)

This reveals a critical distinction. AI can function as an exoskeleton, extending what someone can accomplish while the technology is available. But that improvement does not automatically become an internal skill that remains when the assistance disappears.

The research does not establish that AI prevents learning. It shows that improved performance during AI-assisted work should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of learning. The experiment also involved a specific professional group and set of tasks, so its findings should not be generalised to every learning situation.

*3. Career & Industry Perspective*

*Being Productive Is Not the Same as Being Competent*

This distinction has important implications for careers and education.

Imagine two professionals completing the same data analysis using AI. Both deliver accurate reports. However, one understands the methodology, can identify errors, and can reproduce the analysis independently. The other depends entirely on AI and cannot explain how the results were produced.

Their immediate outputs may look identical, but their underlying capabilities are different.

As organisations integrate AI into their workflows, professionals must develop both the ability to use AI and the knowledge required to evaluate its work. Otherwise, productivity improvements may create hidden dependencies.

For educational institutions, the challenge is similar. Assessments must increasingly distinguish between what learners can accomplish with AI and what they genuinely understand themselves.

The objective should not be to avoid AI assistance. It should be to ensure that assistance contributes to meaningful learning rather than replacing the learning process.

*4. What We Should Learn*

Capability of the Week

**Independent Learning**

This week, we should intentionally develop the ability to turn assisted performance into independent capability.

Whenever we use AI to complete something unfamiliar, we should go beyond accepting the final output.

Understand the process. Examine the reasoning. Identify the concepts behind the result. Practise the task independently and test whether we can reproduce what we learned without assistance.

For example, if AI helps us analyse a dataset, we should also understand the calculations, interpret the findings, and attempt a similar analysis independently.

AI should help us move beyond our current limitations, but we should not allow those limitations to remain permanently hidden behind AI assistance.

The goal is not simply to accomplish more with AI.

**The goal is to become more capable because we learned through using it.**

*5. Reflection*

Technology has always helped humans accomplish things beyond their individual physical and intellectual capabilities. But the purpose of education has never been limited to completing tasks. It has also been about developing the understanding and judgment that allow people to act independently.

AI makes this distinction more important than ever. We can now produce results that demonstrate capabilities we may not personally possess.

**If you can successfully complete a task using AI but cannot explain or reproduce the work independently, would you consider yourself to have learned that skill?**
