*Module 01 – Turning AI Assistance into Lasting Capability*

*Chapter 01 – Recognising the Difference Between Performance and Learning*

*Understanding*

AI can help us successfully complete tasks that are beyond our current knowledge or experience. We may analyse unfamiliar data, write code we could not create independently, or work through a technical problem with AI guidance. The result can be useful and correct, but successful performance does not automatically mean that learning has taken place.

This distinction matters because performance describes what we can accomplish in a particular situation, while learning changes what we understand and can do ourselves. The research highlighted in this week's Weekly Intelligence found that AI assistance improved performance on unfamiliar technical tasks, but found no evidence that those gains became stronger independent technical knowledge afterwards. This does not mean AI prevents learning. It means we should not mistake assisted success for evidence that a skill has become our own.

*Practice*

Think about one unfamiliar task you recently completed with AI assistance. Instead of looking only at the quality of the result, ask yourself: What part of this could I explain clearly without AI? What decisions could I make independently? Which parts would I struggle to reproduce if the assistance disappeared?

We do not need to stop using AI to answer these questions. The purpose is to recognise the difference between what the tool helped us accomplish and what we actually learned through the process.

*Looking Ahead*

Recognising this difference gives us a better starting point for learning with AI. Tomorrow, we will look beyond the final output and examine the process behind it, because understanding how something was accomplished is one step toward turning assistance into lasting capability.

*Looking Back*

This morning, we separated two ideas that can easily appear identical: performing successfully and actually learning. AI can extend what we are able to accomplish while it is available, but the quality of an assisted result alone cannot tell us how much knowledge or capability we have developed.

That distinction gives us a more useful way to evaluate our progress. Instead of asking only, “Did I complete the task?”, we can also ask, “What became part of my own understanding while completing it?”

*Thinking Together*

Assistance itself is not the problem. We use books, teachers, colleagues, calculators, search engines, software, and many other tools to extend what we can do. What matters is whether we remain aware of where assistance ends and our own understanding begins.

When we can explain an idea, recognise mistakes, make informed decisions, and gradually perform more of the process ourselves, assistance can become part of learning. When we can only reproduce the final result while remaining dependent on the tool, our performance may have improved without the same improvement in our capability.

*Moving Forward*

As we use AI this week, we can pay attention to that boundary without rejecting the assistance itself. Tomorrow, we will examine the process behind an AI-assisted result and identify what we need to understand if we want the capability to remain with us.
